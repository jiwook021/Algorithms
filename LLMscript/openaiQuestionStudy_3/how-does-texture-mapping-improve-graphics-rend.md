Imagine you're painting a wall.  You could just paint it a single, solid color (like plain beige).  That's simple, but boring.  Or, you could use wallpaper with a pattern – flowers, bricks, wood grain – to make it look much more realistic.  Texture mapping is like applying that wallpaper to 3D objects in a computer game or movie.

Here's how it works, step-by-step:

1. **The 3D Model:** First, you have a 3D model of an object. Think of it as a wireframe skeleton –  it defines the object's shape but is very plain.  It's like the bare wall before painting.

2. **The Texture:**  Separately, you have a 2D image – this is the "wallpaper." It could be a picture of wood, stone, a face, or anything else. This image is the *texture*.

3. **Mapping the Texture:** The computer program "wraps" or "paints" this 2D texture onto the surface of the 3D model. It's like carefully applying wallpaper to each part of the wall, making sure the pattern matches and doesn't stretch weirdly.  This process is called *mapping*.

4. **Realistic Detail:**  Because you've added a detailed texture, the 3D model suddenly looks far more realistic.  A plain grey cube becomes a realistic wooden chest, a flat plane becomes a cobblestone road, and a simple sphere becomes a detailed planet.

5. **Improved Visuals:** The added detail significantly improves the overall visuals. The scene appears richer, more immersive, and far less artificial.


In short, texture mapping takes a flat image (the texture) and cleverly applies it to a 3D object, giving it realistic surface detail and significantly improving the visual quality of the rendered image.  Without it, 3D graphics would look very bland and blocky.
